# User research 

## Target audience
The primary audience for my story is high school administrators and teachers who are involved in decisions about how generative AI should be used in classrooms. My goal is to communicate the experimental findings in a way that is understandable to educators who may not have much experience interpreting statistical research. I want them to understand my conclusion and recommendation to take a beat and evaluate the real improvement (or lack thereof) in learning through generative AI tools, and decide the best way to use it for their specific students. 

## Interview script

## Research Objectives
My user research will be focused on 5 goals:
1. Make sure that readers understand the main message
2. Identify which visualizations are working best and which need improvement
3. Understand whether the progression of the story makes sense
4. See if the data seems credible/trustworthy
5. Identify any other changes to improve the clarity of the Shorthand story

## Identifying participants
I plan to interview at least 3 people with familiarity using education technology either as a student or a teacher/administrator. I will make sure that if I am unable to find people who meet that criteria, that I am able to find people who have experience using generative AI to complete a task or help them with something they were trying to learn. I will document their backgrounds without revealing any PII, and acknowledge potential limitations.

## Interview findings

| Evaluation Question | Interview 1 | Interview 2 | Interview 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Story clarity | Introduce the distinction between assisted performance and independent learning earlier so readers know what to look for. | Felt the story had a strong overall structure and progression. | Make the transition from the practice results to the independent assessment more dramatic to emphasize the central finding. |
| Visualization effectiveness | Thought the statistics were good but suggested adding more graphics. | Use consistent colors, scales, and labels across the practice and assessment charts so the comparison is easier to interpret. | Suggested using graphs that show more movement and change. Found the scatterplots and dot visualizations difficult to interpret. |
| Depth of analysis | Recommended exploring student aptitude or learning outcomes over time. Mentioned that many professors evaluate student performance using their own scores. | Explain the difference between the descriptive averages and the study's adjusted 17% finding to make the analysis deeper. | Add more explanation of what each chart measures, especially the distinction between student perceptions and actual assessment results. |
| Actionable recommendations | Explain how teachers could use short independent assessments to determine whether AI-assisted practice is improving understanding. | Suggested making the final call to action more emphatic and specific. Recommended considering assistive and adaptive educational technology as alternatives or complements to general-purpose AI. | End with a concise visual summary of the three recommendations so the audience leaves with clear next steps. |
| Overall improvement | Increase visual variety and explore additional data that demonstrates learning over time. | Strengthen the final recommendations to give educators clearer next steps. | Improve chart readability and use more engaging visualizations that communicate changes clearly. |


# Identified changes for Part III
| Research Synthesis | Anticipated Changes for Part III |
|---|---|
| The overall structure of the story was strong. | Keep the existing structure, beginning with AI adoption, introducing the experiment, revealing the difference between practice and exam performance, and ending with recommendations for educators. Focus on improving the visuals and transitions. |
| Statistics were effective, but more graphics would clarify the story | Replace explanations in Sections 2, 9, and 11 with visual summaries. Create a graphic showing that 54% of U.S. teenagers have used AI for schoolwork, a large annotated visual highlighting the study's adjusted 17% negative effect, and a comparison graphic showing the gap between the number of AI education studies and high-quality causal studies. |
| Visualizing student aptitude over time could strengthen the story. | Investigate whether the original research includes longitudinal measures of student learning. If available, create a line chart showing learning outcomes across multiple assessments. If not, keep the current practice versus independent assessment comparison and explain that the study does not investigate long-term changes in aptitude. |
| Professors often evaluate learning using their own student performance scores. | Add a short section after the practice-versus-exam comparison explaining why educators should evaluate students using independent assessments rather than relying exclusively on AI-assisted homework scores. Include a specific example of how a teacher could compare practice performance with an assessment completed without AI. |
| The final call to action was not specific enough. | Replace the current recommendation with three concrete actions: (1) prioritize AI tools that guide students through problems, (2) administer independent assessments to measure understanding, and (3) pilot AI tools against a no-AI comparison group before adopting them across classrooms. Present these actions as three clearly labeled visual takeaways in the final Shorthand section. |
| Consider assistive and adaptive educational technologies. | Research examples of adaptive learning platforms and other assistive educational tools that provide personalized practice, targeted feedback, or step-by-step support. Add a comparison in the recommendations section showing how these approaches differ from general-purpose AI chatbots. Only recommend specific tools where supporting research demonstrates benefits for learning. |
| Some scatterplots and dot visualizations were difficult to understand. | Replace the student learning perceptions dot plot in Section 8 with a horizontal bar chart showing the percentage of students in each group who reported substantial learning. Use direct percentage labels, consistent group colors, and an annotation explaining why these responses differ from independently measured learning outcomes. |
| Charts showing more movement could improve engagement. | Use Shorthand effects to progressively reveal the results in Sections 5–7. First display the practice scores (28.4%, 47.4%, and 66.9%), then transition to the independent exam scores (32.1%, 28.7%, and 31.5%). Keep the same group colors and 0–100% scale so readers can recognize how the apparent AI advantage disappears when assistance is removed. |
